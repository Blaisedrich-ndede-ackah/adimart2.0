# The 30 Laws of UX — short working summaries

Paraphrased from [lawsofux.com](https://lawsofux.com) by Jon Yablonski (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). These are heuristics, not physics. When a law fights a real user need, name the tradeoff. Read the site or *Laws of UX* (O'Reilly) for the full essays.

1. **Aesthetic-Usability Effect.** People judge a pleasing interface as easier to use, and they tolerate small flaws in it. Polish helps. It does not replace a usability check.
2. **Choice Overload.** Too many equal options slow decisions and lower satisfaction. Filter, recommend, or stage the choice.
3. **Chunking.** Break long information into meaningful groups (headings, spacing, grouped digits). People remember chunks better than a raw string.
4. **Cognitive Bias.** People take mental shortcuts (anchoring, confirmation, and others). Use that knowledge to reduce mistakes. Do not use it to manipulate.
5. **Cognitive Load.** Working on a task has a cost. Cut decoration and redundant steps (extraneous load). Keep the load that *is* the task (intrinsic load).
6. **Doherty Threshold.** Productivity jumps when the system answers within about 400 ms. If work takes longer, show a skeleton, progress, or an optimistic update.
7. **Fitts's Law.** The time to hit a target depends on its size and distance. Make frequent actions large and close. Keep destructive actions away from them. Touch targets at least 44×44 px.
8. **Flow.** Deep focus happens when the challenge matches skill, feedback is immediate, and interruptions are few. Don't break a cashier's sale with unrelated chrome.
9. **Goal-Gradient Effect.** People speed up as they near a visible finish. Show progress, and show some progress already made.
10. **Hick's Law.** Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. Fewer, clearer options. Do not hide the meaning of a choice just to shorten the list.
11. **Jakob's Law.** People spend most of their time in *other* products, so they expect familiar patterns (logo home, cart, standard forms, familiar checkout). Be novel only where it earns its place.
12. **Law of Common Region.** Items inside a shared border or background are read as a group. Use a region when proximity alone is not enough.
13. **Law of Proximity.** Things that are close are related. The gap inside a group should be smaller than the gap between groups.
14. **Law of Prägnanz.** People see the simplest interpretation of a shape or layout. Prefer simple forms that say one thing.
15. **Law of Similarity.** Items that look alike are assumed to work alike. One style means one behavior. Links and buttons must not look like plain text.
16. **Law of Uniform Connectedness.** Lines, arrows, or a shared visual thread group items more strongly than color or nearness alone. Use them for sequences.
17. **Mental Model.** People arrive with a picture of how a thing works, built from past products. Match that picture, or teach the new one in the moment of use.
18. **Miller's Law.** Working memory holds a small number of items (often cited as 7±2, and it varies). Chunk; do not use the number as a hard cap on lists or nav.
19. **Occam's Razor.** Prefer the design that solves the task with the least extra. Stop when removing anything else would hurt the job.
20. **Paradox of the Active User.** People skip manuals and start doing the task. Put guidance in the control (placeholder, empty state, inline hint), not in a tour they will dismiss.
21. **Pareto Principle.** A small share of features carries most of the use. Make the frequent path excellent before polishing the long tail.
22. **Parkinson's Law.** Work expands to fill the time allowed. Shorten forms, prefill, and default so the task ends when the person expects it to.
23. **Peak-End Rule.** People remember the most intense moment and the ending, not the average. Design the peak (payment, error, first success) and the last screen on purpose.
24. **Postel's Law.** Accept messy input (spaces in a phone number, extra whitespace) and normalize it. Emit clean, consistent output. Be strict only at a real boundary, and say what that boundary is.
25. **Selective Attention.** People notice what serves their goal and ignore the rest, including things that look like ads or decoration. Put critical facts in the working area.
26. **Serial Position Effect.** Items at the start and end of a list are remembered better than the middle. Put the important actions first and last.
27. **Tesler's Law.** Some complexity cannot be removed, only moved. Default to the system carrying it (format the number, remember the store, compute the total).
28. **Von Restorff Effect.** The item that looks different is the one people remember. Emphasize one thing per view. If everything shouts, nothing does.
29. **Working Memory.** People hold little in mind across steps. Show the total, the customer, and the receipt on the screen that needs them. Do not make them remember.
30. **Zeigarnik Effect.** Unfinished tasks stay in mind. A saved draft or a visible incomplete state helps people return. Do not nag.
